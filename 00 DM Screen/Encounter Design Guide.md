## Encounter Design Guide

This is a personal design reference, not a checklist. Use it to build encounters that hold up at the table.

Every combat falls into one of three tiers. Figure out which tier you're building, then use the questions for that tier. Higher tiers include everything from the tiers below them.

### Tier 1—Filler Encounters

*Random encounters, patrols, minor obstacles. These exist for pacing, resource drain, or atmosphere. They don't need to be memorable—they need to not waste time.*

**Before you prep, ask:**

- What is this encounter *for*? (resource tax, world-building, foreshadowing, pacing between story beats)
- Can this be resolved in 3 rounds or fewer?
- Is there a reason to run this as a combat at all, or would a quick skill challenge or narrative beat serve the purpose better?

**Design baseline:**

- One enemy type, simple tactics (attack nearest, flee at half HP, etc.)
- No secondary objectives needed
- No map required unless terrain matters tactically
- Keep stat blocks to things you can run from memory or with minimal reference

**The only rule:** Even filler fights should have *one concrete detail*—an unusual enemy behavior, a terrain feature, a sensory detail—that makes them feel like they exist in the world rather than in a game.

---

### Tier 2—Standard Encounters

*Planned fights that matter to the session. The party should feel tested and have to make real decisions, but you're not building a centerpiece.*

Everything from Tier 1, plus:

#### What Do They Want?

Before stat blocks, define two things:

1. **What does the party need to accomplish?** If it's just "kill everything," add one of these:
   - A timer (reinforcements coming, ritual completing, building collapsing)
   - Something to protect (NPC, object, escape route)
   - Something to reach or disrupt (far side of the room, a mechanism, a caster)

2. **What do the enemies want?** Enemies with goals fight differently than bags of hit points. Even simple goals change the encounter:
   - *Hold this position* → they use cover, don't overextend
   - *Capture the caster* → they grapple and drag
   - *Buy time for the boss to escape* → they block chokepoints and disengage
   - *Survive* → they flee when outmatched

#### Does This Survive Contact?

Run this quick stress test:

- **Can the party end this in round 1 by focusing fire on one target?** If yes, you need either more independent threats or an objective that can't be solved by damage.
- **Does the enemy side get at least two meaningful actions per round?** Count attacks, spells, and reactions. If the party gets 8+ actions and the enemies get 3, the math is already over.
- **Is there a reason anyone would move after round 1?** If everyone is in position after the first turn, the fight becomes a stationary damage race. Add a reason to reposition—shifting hazards, a moving objective, enemies that disengage and reposition.

#### Pick One Complication

Don't stack these. Choose *one* that fits the encounter's fiction:

- **Terrain pressure.** Difficult terrain, elevation, cover, narrow passages, unstable footing. Changes positioning math without adding stat blocks.
- **Environmental hazard.** Ongoing area damage, obscured vision, collapsing structures, magical interference. Creates urgency without adding enemies.
- **Split attention.** Enemies in two or more groups that can't all be engaged at once. Forces the party to prioritize.
- **Information gap.** Hidden enemies, unclear enemy count, ambiguous threat. The party can't optimize what they can't fully assess.

### Tier 3—Setpiece Encounters

*Boss fights, climactic moments, encounters that should be talked about afterward. These deserve real prep time.*

Everything from Tiers 1 and 2, plus:

#### Layer the Threat

Setpieces need enough going on that the fight can't be reduced to a single tactical problem. Build in **two or three** of the following—not all of them:

- **Staggered threats.** Not everything is on the field at once. Reinforcements, phase transitions, or emerging hazards keep the encounter evolving past round 2.
- **Competing objectives.** The party has to choose between two things they want. Save the hostage or stop the ritual. Kill the boss or close the portal. Protect the NPC or press the attack.
- **The turn 2-3 shift.** Something changes the tactical picture mid-fight. The terrain transforms, hidden enemies reveal, the objective moves, or the enemy changes tactics. This is the single best tool against round-1-delete syndrome.
- **Action economy pressure.** Legendary actions, lair actions, or multiple coordinated enemies that create more decision points per round than the party can cleanly answer.
- **Reaction competition.** Multiple simultaneous triggers that compete for the party's limited reactions. Opportunity attacks coinciding with ranged threats. A shove and a spell in the same window.

#### Boss Design Minimums

If there's a single boss creature:

- It needs **at least two turns per round** (legendary actions, lair actions, or a reaction that matters). One turn per round against a full party is death.
- It needs a way to **avoid or survive focus fire** for at least 2 rounds. This can be mechanical (resistances, high AC, legendary resistance) or tactical (cover, mobility, minions that absorb attention).
- It needs **something it does besides damage.** Control, terrain manipulation, summoning, debuffs—anything that forces the party to react rather than just trade hits.

#### Pacing the Encounter

- **Round 1** should establish the situation. The party sees what they're dealing with and makes their opening moves. The enemies demonstrate their tactics and capabilities.
- **Round 2-3** is where the shift happens. Something changes. This is where the encounter should feel most dangerous and uncertain.
- **Round 4+** is resolution. The party has enough information to execute a plan. If the fight is dragging here without feeling tense, something in the design isn't working.

### The Delete Test

After you've designed an encounter, run this:

> *If the party wins initiative and deals their full round-1 damage to the most obvious target, does the encounter still function?*

If the answer is no, go back and add structure—not hit points. Stagger threats, add a secondary objective, introduce a turn 2 change, or spread enemies so focus fire requires repositioning.

Then run the reverse:

> *If the enemies win initiative and get a great round, is anyone at risk of dying before they act?*

If yes, that's fine for Tier 3—that's what makes it tense. For Tier 2, dial it back. For Tier 1, definitely dial it back.

---

### Post-Session Diagnostic

When a fight didn't land, ask:

- Did the party win initiative and focus fire with clear line of sight on a single target? → **Structural problem.** The encounter had no protection against alpha strike.
- Did the fight feel like a damage race with no decisions? → **Objective problem.** There was nothing to do except attack.
- Did it end before the enemies did anything interesting? → **Pacing problem.** The interesting elements came too late or were too fragile.
- Did the party feel no tension even though they took damage? → **Stakes problem.** Damage without consequences isn't threatening. What were they at risk of *losing*?
- Did it drag on without feeling dangerous? → **Efficiency problem.** Too much HP, not enough threat per round.

These patterns repeat. Track which ones come up most often for your table—that's where your design instincts need calibration.
